## 1. Curation rationale

The corpus exists to support intent, sentiment and priority triage of retail
banking support tickets in Sri Lanka's three working languages, plus the two
romanized varieties that customers actually type. No such resource existed: the
available Sinhala and Tamil NLP resources are news, social media and general
web text, none of which carries banking intents or the code-mixed register that
customer support messages are written in.

## 2. Language varieties

| Track | Variety | Script | Provenance |
|---|---|---|---|
| `english` | English (source) | Latin | BANKING77, unmodified |
| `sinhala` | Sri Lankan Sinhala, colloquial | Sinhala | Machine-translated, hand-corrected |
| `singlish` | Romanized Sinhala | Latin | Rule-based romanisation of the Sinhala track |
| `tamil` | Tamil | Tamil | Machine-translated |
| `tamilish` | Romanized Tamil ("Tanglish") | Latin | Machine-translated romanisation |

**Measured code-mixing.** The Sinhala track is **40.35%** Latin characters by
letter count — English banking loanwords (`card`, `account`, `PIN`) retained as a
Sri Lankan speaker would write them. The Tamil track is **1.26%** Latin.

That asymmetry is a finding, not a formatting detail, and the paper must state
it: the Sinhala hand-correction pass demonstrably preserved code-mixing, and the
Tamil track — which did not receive an equivalent pass — reads as a
monolingual-script rendering of a register that is code-mixed in the wild. Any
cross-track comparison involving Tamil inherits this confound.

`[CONFIRM]` The authors report that translations were manually verified. The
repository states the opposite for Tamil (README: "Tamil Gemini-translated, no
hand pass yet"). Before submission: state who verified, how many rows per track,
against what criteria, and what fraction was changed. The 1.26% figure above is
the quantitative constraint any such statement has to be consistent with.

## 4. Text characteristics

Short first-person customer messages, 9–12 words on average, one intent each, no
conversational turns. Register is informal. Length is stable across tracks
(56.8–76.1 characters), so no track is systematically longer in a way that would
advantage a length-sensitive model.

## 5. Annotation

- **Intent** — BANKING77's own 77-way gold labels, inherited unchanged. Genuine
  human ground truth.
- **Sentiment and priority** — **LLM-generated**, prompt version v8. These are
  *not* human labels, and a classifier trained on them learns to reproduce v8.
  The paper must say this in the abstract, not only in a limitations section.
- **Gold benchmark** — 500 rows independently annotated by a human, used to
  measure how far v8 sits from human judgement.

## 8. Ethical considerations

**No personal data.** BANKING77 utterances are crowdsourced and contain no real
account numbers, names or customer identifiers. Translation and labelling
introduced none. The bank-screenshot images used elsewhere in the project are
synthetically generated.

**Intended use.** Research on multilingual support triage. The corpus is not
sufficient to deploy an automated banking assistant: sentiment and priority
labels are model-generated and carry measured error against human judgement.

**Risk of the deployed setting.** The system this corpus supports decides which
customer reports reach a human. A false `Low` on a fraud report delays a response
where money is actively at risk — the error analysis finds **13.9%** of priority
errors are extreme (`Low` ↔ `High`) rather than adjacent. Any deployment needs a
cost-sensitive operating point and a human review path, not a macro-F1 threshold.

**Language equity.** Performance is not uniform across the five tracks. Tanglish
carries a **+28.9pp** intent error rate over English. Deploying at one global
threshold would deliver systematically worse service to Tamil-speaking customers
who write in Latin script. This belongs in the paper as a limitation, stated in
those terms.
