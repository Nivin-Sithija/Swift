\section{The Corpus}
\label{sec:corpus}

\subsection{Construction}

The corpus holds 13{,}077 retail-banking support tickets in five parallel
renderings, 65{,}385 rows. The English track is BANKING77
\cite{casanueva2020banking77} taken unmodified, so its 77-way intent column is
human gold. The other four derive from it: Sinhala and Tamil by machine
translation, Singlish by rule-based romanization of the Sinhala track, and
Tanglish by machine-translated romanization (Table~\ref{tab:composition}).

The tracks are aligned row for row. A ticket keeps its identifier and all three
labels across all five, so any pair of tracks is a matched sample. That property
carries the experiment in Section~\ref{sec:script} and is why the corpus was
built this way.

\subsection{Split and labels}

We draw one split at the ticket level and fan it across all five tracks, so a
ticket seen in training never appears in test in any rendering. Splitting per
track would place the Sinhala and Singlish versions of one ticket on opposite
sides of the boundary, a leak specific to parallel corpora. The frozen split is
8{,}500 / 1{,}498 / 3{,}079 tickets, or 42{,}500 / 7{,}490 / 15{,}395 rows.

Only intent is human gold. Sentiment and priority were generated by prompting a
large language model over the English text and propagated to the other tracks,
so both carry label noise \cite{northcutt2021labelerrors}. Reporting scores
without reporting what the labels are worth would overstate every result here.
Measured against a 500-ticket human-annotated gold set, sentiment agreement
reaches 0.7931 \negf{} ($\kappa=0.78$) and priority 0.7722 \macrof{}
($\kappa=0.64$). Every score below should be read against those ceilings.

Two distributional facts fix our metrics. Sentiment is 93.7\% Neutral, so
accuracy is uninformative and we report \negf{} throughout. Priority is
53.5 / 36.6 / 9.8 across Low, Medium, and High, so the operationally important
class is the rarest.

\subsection{Two defects in our own corpus}

\textbf{The Tanglish test rendering does not match its training rendering.}
Tanglish intent scores 69.3 \macrof{} on test against 91.7 on development, where
no other track moves more than three points. The cause is vocabulary, not
generalisation: 60.3\% of Tanglish test tokens are unseen in training, against
20--30\% elsewhere. Singlish is generated by a deterministic rule and emits one
spelling per word; Tanglish is machine-translated and emits several, needing
7{,}250 training vocabulary types against Singlish's 3{,}293, with only 39.7\%
of its test types appearing in its own training data. We state this here rather
than only in results, because a reviewer who meets it later will not trust this
section. Every Tanglish test number below is a lower bound and the track is
excluded from every claim the paper rests on.

\textbf{The two native tracks were not built to one standard.} The translation
prompt instructed that English banking vocabulary be left untranslated, which is
how customers write. Over a 31-term banking lexicon, the Sinhala track retains
64.6\% of those terms in English and Tamil 7.6\%; the Latin fractions in
Table~\ref{tab:composition} say the same. Only the Sinhala track implements the
code-mixing the documentation describes. Two consequences bind
Section~\ref{sec:script}: register claims hold for one track of two, and the
Tamil--Tanglish pair is not a second measurement of the Sinhala--Singlish pair,
because the two pairs do not share a baseline.
